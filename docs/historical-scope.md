# Where the LLM story begins

## Decision

LLM Lineage opens with a short prologue on A. A. Markov’s 1913 statistical analysis of dependence in literary text. Its main account begins with Claude Shannon’s 1948 paper “A Mathematical Theory of Communication” and treats his 1951 “Prediction and Entropy of Printed English” as the second half of the opening sequence.

If one year must label the beginning, it is **1948**.

This is a narrative boundary, not a claim that earlier probability, linguistics, logic, computation, or neural ideas were unimportant. It is also not a claim of a documented Markov-to-Shannon-to-modern-LLM causal chain. The boundary marks the point at which the concepts needed for a continuous account—conditional sequence probability, generated approximations to language, entropy, context, and prediction—come together in a directly inspectable primary source.

## Prologue: dependence in text

Markov’s 1913 study examined a 20,000-letter excerpt of Pushkin’s *Eugene Onegin* as a chain of dependent events. The published English translation shows a deliberately narrow abstraction over letters used to test dependence.

That makes the study an unusually concrete prologue: written language is treated as a measurable sequence whose adjacent events are not independent. It does **not** make Markov’s study a modern language model. The work did not learn a predictor, model word meaning, or present text generation as its objective. Its place in the atlas is conceptual ancestry, not an asserted direct influence edge.

Primary record: [Markov, “An Example of Statistical Investigation of the Text Eugene Onegin Concerning the Connection of Samples in Chains”](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0269889706001074), lecture delivered in 1913 and published in English translation in 2006.

## Main opening: language as a stochastic and predictable source

Shannon’s 1948 paper provides the formal spine. It defines discrete information sources and entropy, describes probabilities conditioned on preceding symbols, generalizes the construction to longer contexts, and in Section 3 generates a series of increasingly structured approximations to English at letter and word levels.

The 1951 paper turns predictability into an empirical experiment. A person guesses the next letter with preceding text available; Shannon then connects ranked prediction behavior to bounds on the entropy of printed English. This is not machine learning, but it makes the relationship between context, next-symbol uncertainty, and prediction directly observable.

Together the papers support an opening chapter with both a formal model and a teachable experiment:

- 1948: What does it mean to model a language source probabilistically?
- 1951: How does more preceding context change uncertainty about the next symbol?

Primary sources:

- Shannon, “A Mathematical Theory of Communication,” [Part I](https://doi.org/10.1002/j.1538-7305.1948.tb01338.x) and [Part II](https://doi.org/10.1002/j.1538-7305.1948.tb00917.x), especially Sections 2–3 and 6–7.
- [Shannon, “Prediction and Entropy of Printed English”](https://doi.org/10.1002/j.1538-7305.1951.tb01366.x), especially Sections 3–6.

## Why later landmarks are not the beginning

Later dates make useful act boundaries, but each presupposes part of the story the atlas needs to explain.

| Candidate boundary | What it contributes | Why it is not the atlas’s beginning |
|---|---|---|
| 1954 — Zellig Harris, “Distributional Structure” | A rigorous linguistic program for describing elements through their environments. | This is an important linguistic branch, not a next-symbol model or the invention of contemporary vector embeddings. Treating it as the sole origin would lose the probability-and-prediction spine. |
| 1986 — Geoffrey Hinton, “Learning Distributed Representations of Concepts” | A network learns internal distributed features from relational propositions and uses them to generalize within one small domain comprising two isomorphic family trees. | It is a representation-learning branch, not a word model or language model. Its held-out test contains four relation instances, and the paper explicitly limits its generalization account to particular domains. |
| 1986 — Rumelhart, Hinton, and Williams, “Learning representations by back-propagating errors” | A widely influential demonstration that multilayer networks can learn useful internal representations through error-driven weight updates. | It is an enabling neural-learning branch, not a language-modeling paper. Earlier incarnations of reverse-mode differentiation are documented in the historical literature, so the atlas will not call this paper the unqualified invention of backpropagation. |
| 1988/1990 — Jeffrey Elman, “Finding Structure in Time” | Copies the previous hidden state into a recurrent context and uses next-item prediction to organize task-dependent sequence representations. | It studies generated toy sequences rather than a natural-language probability model. Its word outputs are not normalized, its memory is practically bounded, and its hidden-to-context copy weights are fixed even though the context-to-hidden path is trained. |
| 1991 — Risto Miikkulainen and Michael Dyer, “Natural Language Processing With Modular PDP Networks and Distributed Lexicon” | Learns a shared distributed word lexicon across recurrent sentence-role and scripted-story modules. | It is a supervised neural NLP branch, not a normalized sequence model: expectations are averaged role/filler activations, the data are synthetic scripts, and the modules train with correct intermediate patterns. |
| 1993 — Jürgen Schmidhuber, “\"Neural\" Redundancy Reduction for Text Compression” | Couples a fixed-window neural next-character estimator to arithmetic coding, reports a held-out newspaper test, and explicitly credits Stefan Heil’s collaboration on the application. | It is modeled as a separate antecedent to the later two-author family because title, authorship, venue, codec coverage, test set, results, and runtime differ; the shared mechanism does not justify silently merging the works. |
| 1994/1996 — Jürgen Schmidhuber and Stefan Heil, “Sequential Neural Text Compression” | Trains a fixed-window neural next-character predictor and uses its probability estimates for synchronized lossless coding. | It is an important character-level branch, not the beginning of conditional language modeling: Shannon’s probability-and-prediction spine is already in place, and this work’s squared-error objective, post-hoc sum normalization, external frozen model, and compression evidence differ from later word-vector softmax models. |
| 1995/1997 — Sepp Hochreiter and Jürgen Schmidhuber, “Long Short-Term Memory” | Defines a constant-error memory path with multiplicative input and output gates, then evaluates long-lag learning on artificial sequence tasks. | It addresses recurrent temporal-credit failure rather than beginning language modeling. The original cell has no learned forget gate or cell-state-to-gate feedback links, the efficient gradient is deliberately truncated, and the experiments do not use natural language, speech, or another real-world sequence. |
| 1999/2000 — Felix Gers, Jürgen Schmidhuber, and Fred Cummins, “Learning to Forget: Continual Prediction with LSTM” | Replaces the original LSTM cell’s fixed self-connection with a learned forget gate so state can be retained, decayed, or reset on continual streams. | It extends the original gated-memory mechanism rather than introducing a language model. The report, ICANN, and journal equations and results differ, and the experiments use synthetic continual tasks rather than natural language. |
| 2000 — Wei Xu and Alex Rudnicky, “Can Artificial Neural Networks Learn Language Models?” | Evaluates a normalized direct-softmax bigram with held-out early stopping against count-model baselines. | It is an independent neural-language-model branch with only one input word, no hidden layer, and no learned word features. Its class-level travel-domain result and linear smoothing surrogate do not supply the broader representation-and-context turn. |
| 2000/2003 — Bengio et al., “A Neural Probabilistic Language Model” | Jointly learned continuous word features and a neural conditional probability model, with a three-author NIPS version followed by the canonical four-author JMLR treatment. | It is a strong boundary for the modern neural-language-model act, not the full history. Section 1.2 explicitly notes earlier neural language modeling and related representation work, and the strongest reported JMLR results mix neural and trigram probabilities. |
| 2002 — H. Schwenk and J.-L. Gauvain, “Connectionist Language Modeling for Large Vocabulary Continuous Speech Recognition” | Extends a projection-based neural language model into a 64,000-word speech recognizer through a probability-preserving 2,000-word shortlist and sequential reuse of outputs for the same context. | It operationalizes an existing neural-language-model direction in one hybrid decoder. The network handles about 47% of requested probabilities, WER changes are small, and the paper itself says the evidence does not support strong conclusions. A retrospectively cited 2001 LIMSI report remains an unbound source boundary because no complete text was located and archived author records disagree on authorship. |
| 2002 — Felix Gers, Nicol Schraudolph, and Jürgen Schmidhuber, “Learning Precise Timing with LSTM Recurrent Networks” | Adds weighted cell-state-to-gate peepholes so gates can inspect protected state when learning precise event timing. | Peepholes expose state; they do not replace the separate forget gate that controls retention. The paper uses one-cell synthetic timing tasks, reports no clear general advantage on its multiple-sequence-discrimination task, and does not evaluate language modeling. |
| 2010 — Mikolov et al., “Recurrent neural network based language model” | Combines a named Elman-style recurrent state with a normalized word softmax, speech-recognition rescoring, ensembles, and optional online test-time adaptation. | It explicitly builds on Elman’s recurrence and addresses the fixed-window limitation it identifies in Bengio’s model, so it is a convergence milestone rather than the start of either lineage. The main training propagates only current-step error, the strongest results add mixtures or ensembles, and the conclusion says the simple recurrence did not capture truly long context. |
| 2012 — Martin Sundermeyer, Ralf Schlüter, and Hermann Ney, “LSTM Neural Networks for Language Modeling” | Applies a recurrent LSTM layer to word prediction, studies projection and class-factored outputs, and interpolates the model with a large Kneser–Ney language model for speech rescoring. | The implemented cell combines the 1997 core with a later forget gate and cell-state-to-gate links. The target cites the two intermediate sources collectively; comparison with their complete texts resolves reference 12 to adaptive forgetting and reference 13 to peepholes as a qualified editorial inference, not as wording or implementation identity supplied by the target. Treebank values are plotted without exact points or an identified evaluation split, and the French recognition gain comes from interpolation with a much larger count model. |
| August 2013–June 2014 — Alex Graves, “Generating Sequences With Recurrent Neural Networks” | Trains stacked recurrent networks for next-step text and pen-motion generation, then conditions handwriting on a character sequence through an unnormalized mixture of Gaussian windows whose positive cumulative location increments force every center forward. | The title is represented as a five-revision arXiv preprint family because no inspected record establishes a peer-reviewed venue. Graves calls the device a *window layer*, *soft window*, *window weight*, or *convolutional mechanism*, not attention. The handwriting losses reuse the official test partition in training; presentation-based blind taste tests lack a released protocol, participant count, and judgments; only Figure 18’s primed samples are explicitly selected for legibility. |
| June–October 2014 — Cho et al., “Learning Phrase Representations using RNN Encoder–Decoder for Statistical Machine Translation” | Encodes a variable-length source phrase into one vector, decodes a normalized target sequence autoregressively, and defines a reset/update-gated hidden unit. | It combines older recurrent-memory, learned-representation, and normalized-prediction lines rather than beginning them. The reported translation experiment chiefly adds the neural conditional as a score for phrase pairs already in a conventional phrase table; replacing that table and directly generating full translations remain future work. |
| September 2014–May 2016 — Dzmitry Bahdanau, Kyunghyun Cho, and Yoshua Bengio, “Neural Machine Translation by Jointly Learning to Align and Translate” | Gives each target step a fresh softmax-weighted view of bidirectional source annotations and trains the alignment scorer jointly through translation likelihood. | It explicitly extends the fixed-vector encoder–decoder framework rather than beginning sequence modeling. The bottleneck is a conjecture; the WMT’14 comparison changes attention, bidirectionality, and capacity together; the length curves have no reconstructible bins or uncertainty; and four conditioned attention matrices are qualitative rather than a gold alignment evaluation. |
| September–December 2014 — Ilya Sutskever, Oriol Vinyals, and Quoc V. Le, “Sequence to Sequence Learning with Neural Networks” | Uses separate deep LSTM encoder and decoder networks for direct sentence translation, introduces source-sequence reversal as an optimization intervention, and separately tests 1,000-best rescoring. | It applies established LSTM and recurrent-language-model machinery in a fixed-vector transduction system. Its strongest direct row uses five reversed-source models, its best rescoring row still depends on an existing phrase-based system’s candidate list, and the fixed vector leaves the later attention problem open. |
| March–December 2015 — Sainbayar Sukhbaatar, Arthur Szlam, Jason Weston, and Rob Fergus, “End-To-End Memory Networks” | Makes differentiable reads from explicit input/output memory vectors—structurally key/value-like roles—and repeats them across internal hops before prediction. | ArXiv versions 1–3 are QA-only manuscripts titled “Weakly Supervised Memory Networks”; language modeling begins in version 4. The reported hop trend is not monotonic in every row, QA uses synthetic bAbI tasks and at most 50 sentences, Text8 has one run, and the paper warns that smooth lookup may not scale to larger memory. |
| August–September 2015 — Minh-Thang Luong, Hieu Pham, and Christopher D. Manning, “Effective Approaches to Attention-based Neural Machine Translation” | Compares global and local recurrent cross-attention, dot/general/concat score functions, learned local windows, and attentional input feeding in one translation system. | The five-BLEU headline spans several simultaneous changes; the displayed global-plus-feed to local-p/general-plus-feed step is 0.9 BLEU. The 25.9 WMT’15 result is an eight-model ensemble plus unknown replacement, and the corrected concat equation and `p = 0.2`/`D = 10` details occur only in arXiv version 5. |
| January–November 2016 — Jianpeng Cheng, Li Dong, and Mirella Lapata, “Long Short-Term Memory-Networks for Machine Reading” | Places a causal same-sequence attention read over prior hidden and memory tapes inside each LSTM update, then combines it with cross-sequence attention through shallow and gated deep fusion. | The mechanism remains sequential recurrence and evaluates language modeling, sentiment, and NLI rather than reading comprehension. Seven arXiv revisions materially change equations and reported SNLI and sentiment rows; the canonical 86.3 SNLI row must not be replaced by later-omitted revision-only 89.x results. |
| June–November 2016 — Ankur P. Parikh, Oscar Täckström, Dipanjan Das, and Jakob Uszkoreit, “A Decomposable Attention Model for Natural Language Inference” | Factors bidirectional soft alignment into independently computed Attend, Compare, and Aggregate stages without recurrent or convolutional encoders in the core model; an optional same-sentence layer adds relative-distance bias. | The vanilla system ignores word order, the `O(ℓd²)` summary depends on `ℓ < d`, no runtime is measured, and the 86.3→86.8 comparison adds 200K parameters. ArXiv version 1’s intra-attention denominator is corrected only in version 2 and the proceedings. |
| October 2016–March 2017 — Nal Kalchbrenner et al., “Neural Machine Translation in Linear Time” | Builds an attention-free character transducer from a resolution-preserving unmasked dilated-convolution encoder, a masked dilated-convolution decoder, and dynamic unfolding. | It remains an arXiv preprint; its two revisions materially change mechanisms and experiments, version 2 contains unresolved result and unit conflicts, the complexity argument is analytic rather than timed, and generation remains autoregressive. |
| May–August 2017 — Jonas Gehring et al., “Convolutional Sequence to Sequence Learning” | Replaces both recurrent stacks with contiguous gated convolutions, adds learned source and target positions, and gives every decoder layer a dot-product source-attention read. | Training positions parallelize but generation remains autoregressive; fixed-width paths grow linearly with distance. The PMLR volume was published on 17 July before arXiv version 3 on 25 July; three arXiv revisions change reported BLEU rows without changing the core equations. Transformer cites the work for comparison, familiar cross-attention, and a learned-position alternative, not as the cause of core self-attention. |
| 2017 — Vaswani et al., “Attention Is All You Need” | Replaced recurrence and convolution in an encoder–decoder transduction architecture with attention-based context mixing, improving parallelism on the reported translation tasks. | It is an architectural convergence point. Attention, sequence prediction, learned representations, encoder–decoder modeling, and the training objective all have earlier lineages. The paper is not, by itself, the first pretrained large language model. |
| October 2017–June 2018 — Matthew E. Peters et al., “Deep Contextualized Word Representations” | Mixes a context-independent character token layer with contextual states from a bidirectional LSTM language model, freezes that model during supervised training, and learns a separate layer mixture for each task. | It is a recurrent feature-transfer bridge, not a Transformer, masked language model, causal generator, or full-model supervised fine-tuning method. The anonymous OpenReview artifact, two arXiv revisions, proceedings article, and supplement retain their own dates and result boundaries. |
| January–July 2018 — Jeremy Howard and Sebastian Ruder, “Universal Language Model Fine-tuning for Text Classification” | Stages general language-model pretraining, unlabeled target-domain adaptation, and classifier fine-tuning with layerwise rates, a slanted-triangular schedule, gradual unfreezing, concat pooling, and BPT3C. | It evaluates six English classification datasets, not every NLP task. Five arXiv revisions preserve a FitLaM-to-ULMFiT rewrite and three corrections that the canonical ACL article does not incorporate. |
| June 2018 — Alec Radford, Karthik Narasimhan, Tim Salimans, and Ilya Sutskever, “Improving Language Understanding by Generative Pre-Training” | Pretrains a decoder-only Transformer with a causal next-token objective, serializes several labeled task forms as token sequences, and fine-tunes the full model with an optional auxiliary language-model loss. | The official artifact is one twelve-page “Preprint. Work in progress,” not a peer-reviewed paper or a documented revision family. It evaluates twelve English classification, similarity, entailment, and multiple-choice datasets with separate task-specific fine-tuning; several displayed tasks and ablations do not support an across-the-board win. |
| October 2018–June 2019 — Jacob Devlin, Ming-Wei Chang, Kenton Lee, and Kristina Toutanova, “BERT: Pre-training of Deep Bidirectional Transformers for Language Understanding” | Pretrains a Transformer encoder through masked-token and next-sentence objectives over packed text pairs, then adapts all parameters with lightweight task heads. | ArXiv v1, arXiv v2, and the canonical NAACL article are distinct artifacts. The first manuscript lacks later SQuAD 2.0, training-schedule, and masking-ablation material; the paper reports no uncertainty across pretraining runs, and the released repository does not provide the exact original pretraining program or data snapshots. |
| January–July 2019 — Zihang Dai et al., “Transformer-XL: Attentive Language Models Beyond a Fixed-Length Context” | Reuses detached hidden states across language-model segments and replaces ambiguous reused absolute positions with a four-term relative-position attention score. | The cache is finite activation state rather than an LSTM cell, external memory, or full-history gradient path. ArXiv v1 has seven authors, v2 onward has six, generated-text evidence begins only in v3, and RECL and speed ratios retain their model-group and baseline conditions. |
| February 2019 — Alec Radford et al., “Language Models are Unsupervised Multitask Learners” | Scales a GPT-derived causal Transformer on WebText and evaluates whether task strings, demonstrations, likelihood rules, and generation can elicit downstream behavior without task-specific parameter updates. | “Zero-shot” does not consistently mean zero examples; several tasks remain weak or prompt-sensitive. The one technical-report version uses 117M/345M/762M/1542M labels, while later checkpoints use corrected 124M/355M/774M/1558M names and remain separate release artifacts. |
| 19 June 2019–January 2020 — Zhilin Yang et al., “XLNet: Generalized Autoregressive Pretraining for Language Understanding” | Samples autoregressive factorization orders while leaving tokens in natural order, uses target-aware content and query streams to avoid target leakage, and combines that objective with Transformer-XL recurrence, relative positions, partial prediction with span-based target sampling, and relative segment encoding. | It is a convergence and objective-alternative node, not the beginning of bidirectional context, self-attention, recurrence, or pretraining. The June manuscript, canonical NeurIPS article and supplement, and January 2020 revision change training scale, equations, input descriptions, and results; the official code also conflicts with the canonical special-token order. |
| 26 July 2019 — Yinhan Liu et al., “RoBERTa: A Robustly Optimized BERT Pretraining Approach” | Reimplements BERT and audits masking refresh, sequence packing, next-sentence prediction, batch size, byte-level BPE, data, and training duration while retaining the encoder and masked-token family. | It is a training-recipe study, not a new encoder architecture or objective. Dynamic masking and larger batches are not uniformly better in the controlled tables, removing next-sentence prediction is confounded with packing, and the final cumulative rows combine multiple recipe and data changes. |

## Narrative shape

The opening boundary implies a braided history rather than a single inevitable ladder:

1. **Prologue — sequential dependence:** Markov gives a compact demonstration that real text contains measurable dependence.
2. **Probabilistic spine:** Shannon makes language a conditional stochastic source and next-symbol predictability an empirical question.
3. **Converging branches:** distributional linguistics, trainable representations, recurrent sequence models, statistical language modeling, scaling methods, and attention develop with different aims and evidence.
4. **Neural probabilistic turn:** learned word representations and conditional sequence probabilities are trained together at useful scale.
5. **Recurrent neural convergence:** Elman-style state, gated long-short-term memory, and normalized neural word prediction meet in practical language-model and speech-rescoring systems, with trainability, context, attribution, and computational limits still visible.
6. **Monotonic conditioned generation:** Graves combines next-step recurrent generation with a differentiable Gaussian-mixture window over an auxiliary character sequence. Positive location increments make the read strictly forward-moving; the source calls it a window or convolutional mechanism rather than attention.
7. **Concurrent fixed-vector sequence transduction:** Cho et al. condition a gated recurrent decoder through one encoded phrase vector and evaluate that conditional chiefly as a phrase-table score; Sutskever, Vinyals, and Le use separate deep LSTMs for direct translation, reverse source order, and separately rescore 1,000-best lists. These are overlapping but technically and empirically distinct branches.
8. **Decoder-conditioned retrieval:** Bahdanau, Cho, and Bengio extend the Cho framework so each target step retrieves a soft weighted combination of bidirectional source annotations. Their version-1 discussion directly compares that mechanism with Graves’s monotonic traversal and contrasts the functional constraint; it does not establish motivation, derivation, inspiration, or a causal priority claim. The evidence supports the mechanism and a scoped translation comparison, not a universal bottleneck claim or verified word alignment.
9. **Differentiable memory and practical recurrent attention:** Sukhbaatar et al. repeat soft reads over explicit memory before one prediction; Luong et al. compare global and local cross-attention, multiplicative scores, and input feeding inside a recurrent translator. Both are documented extensions of the 2014 branches, and neither is labeled self-attention or recurrence-free.
10. **Transformer hinge and predecessor gate:** Vaswani et al. combine global encoder self-attention, masked decoder self-attention, encoder–decoder attention, projected heads, explicit positions, and position-wise computation into a parallelizable training architecture without erasing inherited objectives or mechanisms. All six required predecessor nodes—Sukhbaatar, Luong, Cheng, Parikh, ByteNet, and ConvS2S—are now promoted. ByteNet supplies the attention-free, dilated-convolution branch that the Transformer explicitly contrasts without documenting adoption. ConvS2S supplies the contiguous-convolution and layerwise cross-attention branch: Transformer directly contrasts its linear path, includes it among familiar encoder–decoder attention mechanisms, benchmarks its version-2 results, and tests its learned positional-embedding alternative. The resulting qualified edge does not make ConvS2S the cause of core self-attention. Lin, Paulus, Kim’s structured-attention work, Extended Neural GPU, Neural GPU, and Britz remain source-only context unless later evidence changes their inclusion value.
11. **Contextual transfer fork:** Both ELMo and ULMFiT can first adapt a language model on unlabeled target-task text. ELMo then freezes its recurrent bidirectional model during supervised training and transfers task-specific mixtures of internal features; ULMFiT transfers the adapted model's parameters into the classifier and fine-tunes the supervised stack with controlled rates and unfreezing. ULMFiT directly cites and contrasts ELMo's fixed-feature route, supporting a noncausal `addresses` edge without resolving conception, derivation, priority, or universal superiority.
12. **Transformer pretraining fork:** Radford et al. carry the Transformer decoder into causal language-model pretraining, then serialize labeled tasks and fine-tune the whole backbone. BERT keeps Transformer self-attention and full-model adaptation but switches to a deep bidirectional encoder trained with masked-token and next-sentence objectives. The papers’ own citations support scoped Transformer, ULMFiT, ELMo, and GPT relationships; they do not make either work the origin of attention, language modeling, pretraining, fine-tuning, bidirectionality, or subword tokenization.
13. **Cross-segment Transformer memory:** Transformer-XL retains masked multi-head self-attention but lets current queries read a detached cache of earlier activations. Relative distances keep reused states positionally coherent across segment boundaries; the mechanism extends the Transformer language-model branch without inheriting LSTM gates or external-memory slots.
14. **Scale and task conditioning without updates:** GPT-2 continues GPT’s causal Transformer route, changes the tokenizer, corpus, normalization, initialization, context length, and scale, then expresses downstream tasks as text. The report’s zero-shot evidence ranges from filtered language-model results to demonstrations, prompts, and weak open-domain tasks, so no-fine-tuning transfer remains distinct from zero-example competence or instruction tuning.
15. **Permutation-factorization convergence:** XLNet changes the order in which targets are factorized and exposed through attention masks without shuffling the token sequence. A query stream identifies which position to predict without seeing its content, while a content stream carries token-bearing states; partial prediction with span-based target sampling, Transformer-XL memory, relative positions, and relative segment encoding make the method a combination of earlier branches rather than an isolated objective claim.
16. **The training recipe becomes an experimental object:** RoBERTa retains BERT’s encoder, masked-token prediction, and full-model task adaptation while testing how masks, packed inputs, next-sentence prediction, batch size, tokenization, data, and duration affect results. Its strongest rows accumulate several changes, so the historical lesson is recipe sensitivity and evidence discipline—not that one ablated switch universally explains the gain.

The corpus must earn every transition. Conceptual similarity alone will not be drawn as causality. Graves explicitly names the Gers, Schraudolph, and Schmidhuber 2002 LSTM formulation, but that edge is limited to the composite recurrent cell and full temporal training. Sutskever likewise names the Graves formulation without inheriting Graves’s window, mixture-density output, experiments, or sampling methods. Bahdanau version 1 directly calls Graves’s window a similar alignment approach and contrasts its monotonic restriction with translation reordering; this supports a documented `addresses` edge with causal language prohibited, not a claim of derivation or inspiration. Cho version 1 was posted before Sutskever version 1, but Sutskever version 1 explicitly describes parallel development and the shared fixed-vector strategy as independently taken. Bahdanau version 1 then appeared nine days before Sutskever version 1 while already citing an anonymized under-review Sutskever manuscript; Sutskever reciprocally cited Bahdanau. The atlas may use public posting dates for display order, but those records do not resolve conception, submission, private circulation, derivation, priority, or influence direction. Accordingly, Cho→Bahdanau is documented from the target’s explicit framework statements, while no Sutskever↔Bahdanau edge is drawn.

The scoped lineage now continues through the completed convergence gate. Hochreiter–Schmidhuber→Cheng covers the named gated recurrent substrate without backdating the forget gate; Sukhbaatar→Cheng covers soft memory addressing and a multi-hop analogy; Bahdanau→Cheng covers cross-sequence attention combined with Cheng’s recurrent tape. Bahdanau→Parikh covers the target’s feed-forward adaptation of soft cross-sentence alignment, and Cheng→Parikh covers only the explicitly attributed intra-sentence-attention concept because the target replaces recurrent tape addressing with all-token feed-forward self-alignment. Cho→ByteNet and Sutskever→ByteNet cover ByteNet’s explicit generalization of the fixed-vector connection pattern; Bahdanau→ByteNet covers only the paper’s direct contrast with attentional pooling. Bahdanau→ConvS2S and Luong→ConvS2S cover recurrent soft cross-attention and multiplicative scoring; Sukhbaatar→ConvS2S is a noncausal multiple-hop analogy. ConvS2S→Transformer is the sole direct incoming Transformer edge and covers only familiar encoder–decoder attention plus the learned positional-embedding alternative. Structural similarities between MemN2N reads and query/key/value roles, Luong’s unscaled score and later scaled dot-product attention, Cheng’s tape and Transformer self-attention, Parikh’s factorization and Transformer layers, or ByteNet’s causal mask and the Transformer decoder mask remain comparisons rather than direct Transformer relationships.

After that hinge, Transformer→GPT covers only the cited masked-decoder substrate; ULMFiT→GPT covers the target’s closest staged-transfer line without importing ULMFiT’s target-domain adaptation or training schedule; and ELMo→GPT records the target’s feature-versus-full-fine-tuning contrast with causal language prohibited. Transformer→BERT covers the cited encoder substrate, GPT→BERT covers BERT’s target-documented comparison and intentionally close fine-tuning design before its switch to deep bidirectional pretraining, and ELMo→BERT records the contextual-feature versus joint full-model route as a noncausal contrast. Transformer→Transformer-XL adds only the target-documented cached-state and relative-position extension, not recurrent gates or external memory. GPT→GPT-2 records the closest model continuity, while the additional Transformer→GPT-2 edge preserves the target’s explicit architectural citation without bypassing GPT’s implementation role. Transformer→XLNet covers the self-attention substrate that XLNet reparameterizes; Transformer-XL→XLNet covers the explicitly integrated recurrence and relative positions; and BERT→XLNet records the target’s scoped objective critique and retained fine-tuning practice. Transformer→RoBERTa covers the encoder substrate, BERT→RoBERTa covers the model family being reimplemented and optimized, and GPT-2→RoBERTa is limited to the target’s explicit adoption of byte-level BPE. No XLNet→RoBERTa edge is needed merely because RoBERTa imports comparator rows and questions objective-only explanations. Every one of these edges includes target-side paper evidence and claims from both endpoints. None encodes exclusive derivation, priority, universal empirical superiority, or checkpoint releases as paper revisions.

This additional recurrent-generation branch does not change the first planned vertical slice. The slice remains Good’s frequency-of-frequencies context followed by Katz backoff, original Kneser–Ney, and Chen–Goodman’s interpolated multi-discount treatment; the Graves material remains a later candidate whose interactions must satisfy their own gates.

Within the audited version families, the citation direction is one-way: Graves’s five revisions do not cite Bahdanau, while Bahdanau cites Graves and supplies the direct comparison. Chronology and citation establish comparison, not inspiration or derivation.

## Boundary test

The 1948 boundary should be reopened if a pre-1948 primary source is found that satisfies all of these conditions:

1. It models natural-language sequences probabilistically.
2. It conditions prediction or generation on prior context.
3. It treats the construction as a predictive or generative source, not only a frequency count.
4. It has evidence of continuity into later language-modeling practice.

Markov may remain a prologue only while removing it leaves the technical spine intact. Shannon belongs in the main account only while removing the 1948–1951 sequence leaves probability, generated language approximation, contextual prediction, and entropy without an equally strong opening source.
