# Corpus format

LLM Lineage keeps research evidence separate from interface copy. The canonical corpus is [data/corpus.json](../data/corpus.json), and its structural contract is [schemas/corpus.schema.json](../schemas/corpus.schema.json).

The current schema is `0.8.0`. As of 2026-08-23, the checked-in corpus contains 33 works, 90 versions, 73 ideas, 206 sources, 387 claims, 44 relationships, and 64 learning objectives.

The checked-in corpus covers Markov’s 1913 *Eugene Onegin* study, Shannon’s 1948 and 1951 papers, Good’s 1953 frequency-of-frequencies estimator as statistical context, Jelinek, Bahl, and Mercer’s 1975 statistical speech decoder, Jelinek and Mercer’s 1980 treatment of interpolation and deleted estimation for sparse Markov sources, and the reciprocally cross-referenced 1982 handbook chapter that applies the method to a word-trigram model. It records the differently titled 1983 IEEE article, with its different author order, as a journal companion to that chapter after a claim-level comparison, adds Hinton’s 1986 learned distributed representations as a neural representation branch, and adds Katz’s 1987 recursive backoff estimator while keeping its inherited Good–Turing machinery distinct from Katz’s stated contribution. Elman’s April 1988 technical report and canonical March 1990 article contribute a prediction-trained recurrent-state branch; Miikkulainen and Dyer’s July 1991 article then builds a separately supervised modular neural NLP system around recurrent state and a task-learned global word lexicon. Its case-role and script outputs are not normalized language-model probabilities. Schmidhuber’s separate 1993 article establishes an earlier neural character-probability and arithmetic-coding node; the 1994–1996 Schmidhuber–Heil family retains that mechanism while adding codecs, tests, and diagnostics. The classical branch continues through Kneser and Ney’s 1995 context-diversity fallback distribution to Chen and Goodman’s 1998 interpolated, multi-discount extension. A separate recurrent branch adds Hochreiter and Schmidhuber’s 1995 technical report and canonical 1997 journal article, which define constant-error memory with input and output gates while keeping the original cell distinct from later learned retention and cell-state-to-gate variants. Gers, Schmidhuber, and Cummins’s 1999/2000 work then replaces the original fixed self-connection with learned state retention and reset. Bengio and Bengio’s 1999/2000 work contributes an ordered neural estimator for high-dimensional joint distributions. Xu and Rudnicky independently evaluate a direct one-word, no-hidden-layer softmax bigram in October 2000; Bengio et al.’s 2000 conference paper and canonical 2003 journal article instead combine learned word representations, a multiword feed-forward predictor, full-vocabulary normalization, the earlier joint-distribution mechanism, and a documented neural/interpolated-trigram mixture. Schwenk and Gauvain’s May 2002 branch explicitly extends that projection-based approach into a hybrid 64,000-word speech recognizer using a probability-preserving shortlist and sequential reuse of outputs requested for the same context. Gers, Schraudolph, and Schmidhuber’s August 2002 paper separately adds cell-state-to-gate peepholes to the forget-gated cell. Mikolov et al.’s September 2010 paper then combines the Elman recurrence it names with normalized neural word prediction, speech-rescoring mixtures, and optional online adaptation, while preserving one-step temporal credit assignment and long-context limitations. Sundermeyer et al.’s September 2012 paper applies a composite cell containing the original gated-memory core, adaptive retention, and cell-state-to-gate links to a word-level model; the correspondence is a qualified cross-source inference rather than evidence of identical implementations. The paper also tests projection and class-factored outputs and interpolates the result with a much larger count model for speech recognition. Graves’s August 2013–June 2014 preprint family combines stacked recurrent next-step generation with mixture-density handwriting prediction and a text-conditioned, unnormalized Gaussian-mixture soft window whose positive cumulative location increments force every window center forward. The corpus preserves Graves’s terms *window layer*, *soft window*, *window weight*, and *convolutional mechanism* rather than backdating the word *attention*. Cho et al.’s June–October 2014 family then contributes a fixed-vector RNN encoder–decoder and reset/update-gated hidden unit, but evaluates the neural conditional chiefly as a phrase-table score inside a conventional statistical translator. Bahdanau, Cho, and Bengio’s seven-revision family replaces one global source summary with decoder-conditioned soft weighting over bidirectional position-wise annotations, while preserving the fixed-vector critique as a conjecture, the attention matrices as qualitative evidence, and its direct Graves comparison as noncausal. Sutskever, Vinyals, and Le’s separate September–December 2014 family uses deep LSTMs for direct sentence translation, source reversal, beam search, and a distinct 1,000-best rescoring experiment while naming the Graves LSTM formulation. Cheng, Dong, and Lapata’s January–November 2016 family retains left-to-right recurrence while letting each token softly read prior hidden and memory tapes; its seven arXiv revisions materially change equations and reported SNLI and sentiment rows. Parikh et al.’s June–November 2016 family instead decomposes NLI into recurrence-free cross-sentence attention, local comparison, and aggregation, with an optional distance-biased intra-sentence layer; its first arXiv equation has a denominator corrected in version 2 and the proceedings. Vaswani et al.’s June 2017–August 2023 family is then recorded as a Transformer convergence node with self- and cross-attention, projected heads, explicit positions, constant-depth global paths, and canonical 28.4/41.0 translation results. Its seven arXiv revisions remain distinct from the 11-page NIPS paper: parsing, attention maps, the two-revision parameter-attention appendix, and later 41.8 reporting do not migrate into the canonical version. These distinct neural NLP, character-compression, direct-bigram, representation-learning, joint-estimation, language-model, recurrent-convergence, gated-memory, decoder-engineering, monotonic sequence-window, fixed-vector transduction, soft-attention, recurrent intra-attention, decomposable attention, and attention-centered transduction mechanisms are not collapsed into one origin story.

Four audited branches now sit between recurrent translation attention and the Transformer convergence record. Sukhbaatar et al. make soft reads from input/output memory vectors trainable from answer or next-word loss and repeat them across internal hops; versions 1–3 retain the earlier title *Weakly Supervised Memory Networks* and omit the later language-model study. Luong, Pham, and Manning compare global and local recurrent cross-attention, multiplicative scores, and input feeding; the official EMNLP paper remains distinct from arXiv version 5, which corrects the concat scorer and adds dropout and local-window hyperparameters. Cheng, Dong, and Lapata place a causal same-sequence attention read over prior hidden and memory tapes inside the LSTM update, then combine it with cross-sequence attention. Parikh et al. remove recurrent and convolutional encoders from their core NLI model by factoring bidirectional soft alignment into independent Attend, Compare, and Aggregate stages, with optional distance-biased feed-forward intra-attention. None of these four records currently has a direct Transformer edge; ByteNet and ConvS2S remain the two unaudited convergence predecessors.

## What the corpus represents

The format separates six record types:

1. **Works** hold a display citation, inclusion rationale, chronology, versions, limitations, and beginner/expert significance. Each version owns its title, ordered authors, and machine-readable bibliography, including structured series, chapter, container editors, publisher, publication places, and identifiers when relevant.
2. **Ideas** name the mechanisms, formalisms, findings, objectives, problems, or constraints that connect works.
3. **Sources** identify the exact publisher record, original text, search snippet, translation, reprint, or archive consulted, with an access date and version scope.
4. **Claims** are atomic statements with a directness label, confidence, source locator, limitations, and uncertainty.
5. **Relationships** connect works or ideas using both a connection type and an independent evidence class.
6. **Learning objectives** define different beginner and expert outcomes over the same work, idea, and claim IDs.

This separation lets the eventual interface change its teaching path without creating a second factual history.

## Dates and versions

Every chronology event records a value and its precision:

```json
{
  "value": "1948-07",
  "precision": "month",
  "calendar": "gregorian"
}
```

A work’s `chronology.orderingEventId` selects the event used for timeline order. Other events remain visible rather than being collapsed into one date. Every event also carries `dateEvidence`; validation rejects a date whose precision is finer than the cited record supports.
Historical dates that have not been calendar-normalized use `source-as-printed` with a note instead of silently pretending to be Gregorian.

Versions are also explicit. An article, book chapter, proceedings paper, technical report, translation, or corrected reprint can have different titles, ordered authors, sources, and pagination without silently sharing an identity or locator. The work-level title, authors, artifact type, and display citation describe the canonical version; validation requires the title and ordered authors to match that version.

The 1983 article is grouped with the 1982 chapter for publication provenance, not represented as another intellectual advance. All eight promoted lineage claims are present in both publications with matching section structure, equation numbering, quantitative values, and substantially the same wording. The grouping does not assert that the authors or publishers called it an edition or reprint, nor that either text derived from the other: the article’s manuscript history and the chapter’s year-only publication date leave development order unresolved.

Chen and Goodman’s 1999 journal article is grouped with the expanded 1998 report because it identifies that report as its extended version and retains the promoted interpolation, multi-discount, implementation, and evaluation claims. The same-title 1996 ACL paper remains outside this version family: its complete text contains neither Kneser–Ney discussion nor the promoted formulas, and the later publications describe it as earlier comparison work.

Bengio and Bengio’s differently titled NIPS, IDIAP, and IEEE records are grouped as one intellectual work after full-text comparison: the publications share a near-identical abstract, factorization, constrained neural architecture, pruning method, four datasets, and table structure, and Bengio et al. 2003 cite the two 2000 publications together as one prior model. The NIPS version reverses the journal author order, and the repository manuscript is modeled as an IDIAP technical-report version because it lacks printed IEEE folios. No primary record explicitly calls the IEEE article an extension, so the grouping is an evidenced editorial judgment and no directed relation is asserted among versions.

Bengio et al.’s same-title conference and journal papers are grouped editorially because they share the first three authors and core three-step mechanism. Cheng, Dong, and Lapata is represented by seven dated arXiv revisions and the canonical eleven-page EMNLP article. ## Evidence locators

Every claim requires at least one source location. Locators use the numbering printed in the cited source, not a browser or PDF viewer’s page index. When an inspected manuscript has no printed folios, its source records a `pdf-page` leaf-count envelope, but promoted locators omit page fields and use stable section, equation, figure, table, or paragraph anchors instead. Validation rejects PDF-position fields on evidence from such a source:

```json
{
  "sourceId": "shannon-1951-full-text",
  "locator": {
    "label": "§5, equation (17), printed p. 61",
    "section": "5. Entropy Bounds from Prediction Frequencies",
    "pageStart": 61,
    "pageEnd": 61,
    "equation": "17"
  },
  "support": "Gives the inequality connecting rank frequencies with upper and lower entropy bounds."
}
```

The `support` field explains why the location is relevant; it is not a substitute for reading the source.

Source provenance is independent of source content. The inspected 1980 facsimile is recorded as a primary full text on a third-party host, while library catalog records remain the preferred bibliographic sources. For the 1982 volume, Internet Archive is metadata-only because its scan is restricted; separately recorded Google Books query-conditioned search snippets supply the inspected claim locators, while the publisher and Crossref deposits remain the preferred bibliographic records. The 1983 claim comparison uses an author-uploaded IEEE scan that retains printed pagination and an institutional-use restriction. The Good and Katz audits likewise use non-preferred reading copies with exact printed pagination alongside Oxford, IEEE, and Crossref metadata. Hinton’s claim audit uses the author-hosted scan alongside the official Cognitive Science Society proceedings and eScholarship record; the scan and proceedings retain copyright notices and are not redistributed. Elman’s journal claims use a UCSD-hosted scan with printed pagination; Wiley and Crossref supply the March/current-DOI metadata, ScienceDirect supplies the April–June issue coverage and legacy DOI, the author bibliography establishes the unavailable superseded report’s identity, and the CRL newsletter alone supplies its April 1988 date. ## Relationship discipline

Relationship direction is earlier or contributing node → later or receiving node. Each edge has:

- a `connectionType`, such as `extends` or `conceptual-parallel`;
- an `evidenceClass`: `documented-influence`, `inherited-mechanism`, or `editorial-synthesis`;
- claim IDs and direct source evidence for every relationship;
- a `causalLanguage` gate for rendering;
- confidence, rationale, and uncertainty.

Only `documented-influence` may opt into carefully qualified causal explanation. Strong causal phrases remain rejected even there: a citation documents a connection, but does not by itself prove that one work caused another. A dated-before-B relationship is not enough.

The current corpus demonstrates the distinction:

## Priority language

Claims containing terms such as “first,” “invented,” “originated,” or “proved” require a `priorityReview` with:

- an operational definition;
- the scope of the prior-art search;
- the sources supporting the conclusion.

Without that record, validation fails. Narrow wording such as “the paper constructs” or “the experiment reports” is preferred when it says exactly what the evidence supports.

## Validation

Run:

```sh
npm run validate:corpus
npm test
```

The validator checks the JSON Schema and cross-record editorial rules, including:

- complete source records and evidence locators;
- valid calendar values and source-supported date precision;
- unique IDs and valid references across works, versions, sources, ideas, claims, and objectives;
- structured version titles and ordered authors, with canonical-version identity matching the work;
- source/work/version scope, evidence pages within the cited source’s pagination, and structural-only locators for unpaginated `pdf-page` sources;
- explicit version-boundary evidence for related sources that intentionally remain outside a work’s version family and cannot support promoted claims, relationships, or chronology;
- controlled relationship and evidence labels;
- direct source evidence for documented influence;
- chronological direction for lineage edges and rejection of unsupported causal or priority wording;
- learning objectives whose cited claims belong to their stated work;
- both beginner and expert objectives for every full-treatment included work; context-only records may remain bounded prerequisites without their own paired experience.

The test suite mutates the valid corpus to prove that these failure cases are rejected.

## Expansion rule

A candidate should not be promoted by adding a title and date alone. A corpus-ready work needs:

- a primary source and version-aware bibliography;
